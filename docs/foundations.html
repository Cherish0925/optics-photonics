What the model means

The formula is an ontology and modelling discipline, not a substitute for the theories underneath it. It tells us what every optical problem is built from and where each approximation enters.

FieldWhat light is doingState, propagation, modes, phase, polarization, coherence, photons, correlations and quantum statistics.
MatterWhat light meetsAtoms, electrons, lattices, semiconductors, biological tissue, gain media, detectors and all structured optical components.
InteractionHow they change each otherAbsorption, emission, refraction, scattering, nonlinearity, gain, detection, force, heating and hybridization.
H = H_field + H_matter + H_int + H_drive + H_env

Free-space propagation is field self-dynamics. Sources, samples and detectors are matter systems coupled to the field. Geometry usually enters through material layout, interfaces, boundaries and ports. Spacetime geometry becomes an additional background degree of freedom when gravitational effects matter.

Field

Classically, light is the electromagnetic field: E(r,t), B(r,t), D(r,t) and H(r,t). Quantum mechanically, the same field is quantized; field observables become operators and modes are described by creation and annihilation operators.

Photon ≠ replacement for the field. A photon is an excitation of the quantized electromagnetic field. Quantum optics does not remove the electromagnetic field; it enlarges the state space and changes how observables and measurements are represented.
Spectral freedomFrequency, wavelength, bandwidth, comb structure.
Spatial freedomWave vector, wavefront, transverse mode, near/far field.
Vector freedomPolarization, helicity, spin angular momentum.
Temporal freedomPulse envelope, delay, phase, repetition structure.
Modal freedomGuided modes, cavity modes, orbital angular momentum.
Quantum freedomPhoton number, squeezing, coherence, correlations, entanglement.

Matter

Matter is every microscopic or effective degree of freedom that can generate, guide, transform, absorb, scatter, amplify or detect light. The relevant variables may be electrons, holes, atomic levels, molecules, phonons, spins, excitons, carrier plasmas or collective states.

At the classical device level, much of this microscopic physics is compressed into response functions such as ε(r,ω), μ(r,ω), σ(r,ω), χ⁽¹⁾, χ⁽²⁾ and χ⁽³⁾. At the microscopic level, those quantities are consequences of a matter Hamiltonian coupled to the electromagnetic field.

An optical device is structured matter engineered to implement a transformation.
ObjectPhysical reading
LensMaterial geometry + refractive response that transforms wavefronts.
Waveguide / fiberStructured dielectric matter that supports selected EM eigenmodes.
MetasurfaceSubwavelength spatial distribution of designed electromagnetic response.
Laser mediumDriven matter that transfers stored energy coherently into optical modes.
DetectorMatter engineered to convert a field state into a stable electronic, thermal, chemical or mechanical record.

Interaction

Interaction is the bridge that produces most familiar optical phenomena.

PhenomenonUnified interpretation
Reflection / refractionBoundary and material response reorganize the field.
Absorption / emissionEnergy moves between field and matter sectors.
Stimulated emissionAn incident field drives coherent radiation from gain matter.
ScatteringMatter changes momentum, phase, polarization or frequency of the field.
Dispersion / birefringenceMaterial response depends on frequency, direction or polarization.
Nonlinear opticsMatter response depends nonlinearly on field amplitude.
Raman / BrillouinLight couples to molecular, vibrational or acoustic degrees of freedom.
Plasmonics / polaritonsField couples strongly to charge or material excitations; hybrid modes emerge.
OptomechanicsOptical and mechanical modes exchange energy and momentum.
DetectionField–matter coupling creates a readable record.

Interference and diffraction are mainly field dynamics: superposition plus propagation. They fit naturally without pretending that matter must be present at every point.

One structure, many theory levels

QED→Effective light–matter Hamiltonians→Maxwell + response→Wave optics→Ray optics

These are not competing descriptions. They are a hierarchy of effective theories. Moving left retains more microscopic degrees of freedom. Moving right discards detail in exchange for simpler equations and a narrower validity domain.

Quantum field theoryElectromagnetic gauge field + charged matter fields + interaction.
Quantum opticsSelected optical modes + emitters / phonons / spins + effective coupling.
Classical electromagnetismMaxwell equations + constitutive relations + sources and boundaries.
Wave opticsHelmholtz, Fresnel, Fourier, paraxial and coupled-mode approximations.
Geometrical opticsEikonal and ray limit when wavelength is much smaller than structural variation.
System opticsTransfer functions, S-parameters, imaging operators and reduced device models.

Disciplinary use of “optics” and “photonics” does not cover all of these bands equally, but the underlying electromagnetic field–matter framework does.

Devices are transformations

input field state → structured matter + interaction → output field / matter state

Every device can be interrogated with five questions: What enters? What matter and geometry are present? What coupling law applies? What state exits or is stored? What observable is read out?

This abstraction lets lenses, waveplates, gratings, fibers, cavities, SLMs, cameras and metasurfaces be compared at the same conceptual level even though their microscopic implementations differ.

Experiments are state-transformation chains

Source→State preparation→Propagation→Sample / device→Detection→Inference / control

This chain is useful from table-top optics to autonomous laboratories. A source prepares a field through matter–field interaction; propagation evolves it; a sample transforms field and matter states; detection creates a record; inference extracts the quantity of interest; feedback changes controls and closes the loop.

Forward and inverse views

(field, matter, coupling) → observable
desired observable / transformation → field preparation + matter geometry + control

Lens design, metasurface synthesis, inverse nanophotonics, pulse shaping, computational imaging and automated experiment design are variations of the inverse form.

Measurement and information stay inside the same physics

A detector is another matter system coupled to the field. What makes it a measurement apparatus is that the interaction creates a stable record. In quantum optics, observables and POVMs describe the statistics of that process; the physical record still comes from an interaction.

Information is encoded in distinguishable states and correlations: amplitude, phase, frequency, polarization, spatial and temporal mode, photon number, coherence and entanglement. Communication, sensing and imaging mainly differ in the prepared state, the channel acting on it and the inference performed at the output.

Where the compact formula needs care

Do not start every problem from QED. Use the simplest model that still contains the decisive physics.
Matter can be active. Gain, pumping, non-equilibrium carriers and nonlinear media are matter dynamics, not exceptions.
Strong coupling creates hybrids. Polaritons and related excitations mix field and matter; the decomposition identifies their parent sectors rather than forcing the eigenstates to separate.
Geometry is usually implementation, not ontology. Interfaces and layout enter through structured matter and boundaries.
Gravity adds spacetime. In gravitational lensing or curved-spacetime QFT, background geometry should be written explicitly.
Discipline names are narrower than physics. Not every EM phenomenon is conventionally called optics, even though the same framework applies.
Optics and photonics study electromagnetic-field states and dynamics, matter states and responses, and the interactions that generate, transform, guide, detect and control them.

A practical modelling checklist

  1. Field. Classical or quantum? Wavelength, bandwidth, modes, polarization, coherence, power or photon statistics?
  2. Matter. Which microscopic or effective degrees of freedom actually matter?
  3. Interaction. Linear, nonlinear, resonant, dissipative, scattering, gain, force or measurement?
  4. Geometry. Dimensions, interfaces, boundaries, periodicity, disorder and ports?
  5. Regime. Near/far field, weak/strong coupling, equilibrium/driven, linear/nonlinear?
  6. Approximation. QED, effective Hamiltonian, Maxwell, Helmholtz, paraxial, coupled mode or rays?
  7. Observable. Field, spectrum, image, force, temperature, current, counts, correlation or state fidelity?
  8. Validation. Conservation laws, limiting cases, numerical convergence, calibration and experimental uncertainty?
